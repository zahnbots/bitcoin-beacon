On Isla Mujeres, Sixteen Shops Now Take Bitcoin

ISLA MUJERES, Mexico — On a limestone island a ferry ride from Cancún, you can now buy a taco, a hotel night, or a golf-cart tour with bitcoin. Two years ago you could not. BTC Isla, a not-for-profit started by Isabella Santos after she moved to the island in early 2024, has onboarded sixteen merchants to take payment over the Lightning Network — up from zero — plus an ATM and point-of-sale kiosks. It charges no fees and runs free classes in a Bitcoin Café. Sixteen is small against several hundred island businesses; the slope is the story.

Why it matters: the adoption that lasts is built shop by shop, not decreed from a capital.

Kazakhstan Takes a 10% Cut of Miners’ Bitcoin

A law effective August 1 requires the country’s largest mines — those over 150 megawatts — to hand a tenth of what they mine each month to a state fund, which routes the coins into a national reserve. In return, miners get capped power on ten-year contracts. It’s a royalty, not a conviction buy. Full story →

Ninety Wallets Now Hold 10,000 Bitcoin Each

The count of wallets holding at least 10,000 BTC has hit 90, a six-month high, up six in eight weeks. Those addresses added a net 46,420 BTC in two months — the fastest accumulation of 2026 — even as smaller holders sold after the Coldcard exploit. Supply is rotating to stronger hands. Full story →

Argentina Lets Its Banks Hold Bitcoin

Under a central-bank framework that reverses a 2022 ban, regulated Argentine banks can now offer bitcoin trading and custody inside the apps millions already use, through separate units held to higher capital rules. In an inflation-scarred country, bitcoin moves from the street market toward the bank branch. Full story →

MARA Borrows $600 Million Against Its Bitcoin

Rather than sell, the miner MARA pledged 18,750 BTC — about $1.2 billion — as collateral for $600 million in new debt from Coinbase and Two Prime. A bet the treasury out-earns the interest, and a tell: even as some firms dump coins, the largest miners are borrowing to keep theirs. Full story →

The Take: The State Found a Cheaper Way to Own Bitcoin

Kazakhstan won’t buy bitcoin — it will take a tenth of what its miners produce. Oman pooled its miners; Pakistan is wiring surplus power to its own rigs. Call it what it is: a royalty, not conviction. A government that extracts coins can also switch the pipeline off, which is exactly why a mined reserve is weaker than a bought one.
