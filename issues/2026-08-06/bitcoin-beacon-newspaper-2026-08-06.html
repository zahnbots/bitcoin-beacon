Kenya and South Sudan Now Send Bitcoin Between Them for Free

A new open-source layer moves value between Kenya’s M-PESA and South Sudan’s MTN MoMo over bitcoin — no routing fees, no custodian off the continent.

NAIROBI — At a hackathon in Nairobi this summer, a handful of African bitcoin firms rewired how money crosses their borders. Tando, which already lets 40 million Kenyan M-PESA numbers receive bitcoin, tore out the Lightning plumbing it rented from a middleman and replaced it with Bark, an open-source build of the Ark protocol.

The switch cut Tando’s liquidity costs by an estimated 50 to 80% — and kept every coin in the operator’s own hands. Now the same rail reaches South Sudan, where a developer built a Tando-style gateway on MTN Mobile Money.

A trader in Juba and a shopkeeper in Nairobi can now move value between two currencies with zero routing fees and no company holding their funds offshore. A Nigerian team is extending it to bank accounts in Lagos, Accra, and Johannesburg. Together the corridors would span five currencies and more than 400 million people.

Why it matters: the cheapest way to move money across Africa is being built in Africa, and it answers to no one off the continent.

The Take · Opinion

Africa Builds Its Own Bitcoin Rails. Asia Licenses Them.

Two visions of “bringing bitcoin onshore” surfaced this week. In Nairobi and Juba, developers wired mobile money to bitcoin on open software that no company or government controls. In Hanoi, the state will permit bitcoin trading in September — but only through five licensed firms, only in dong, behind a $380 million capital wall. Both widen access. Only one leaves the user holding the keys. The case for Hanoi is real: licensed venues bring consumer protection and banks that won’t vanish overnight. But a rail you don’t control is a rail someone else can close.

Network & Mining · Toronto

The hunt begins. The theft that emptied Coldcard wallets keeps climbing — roughly 1,800 coins, about $116 million, drained from more than 5,200 addresses since July 30. Galaxy Digital is now tracing the loot for dozens of victims and says about 90% of it sits unmoved, in addresses it has passed to federal agents and exchanges. Coinkite is helping users file police reports; it has not offered to make anyone whole. Full story →

Policy & Nation-States · Hanoi

A date is set. Vietnam will open a five-year pilot market on September 9, under a resolution signed by Deputy Prime Minister Ho Duc Phoc, with up to five licensed exchanges. The catch: trades settle in dong, only Vietnamese firms qualify, and each needs roughly $380 million in capital. One of Asia’s largest bitcoin-owning populations is being ushered onshore — on the state’s terms. Full story →

Money & Macro · San Salvador

Whose coins are these? El Salvador’s bitcoin reserve has quietly passed 7,700 coins — even though its $1.4 billion IMF loan forbids new public purchases. The government still posts a coin-a-day; the IMF says the rising balance is only wallets being consolidated, not fresh buying. The next review will force the two stories to reconcile, with more than $460 million and a bailout riding on the answer. Full story →
