El Salvador Keeps Buying Bitcoin as IMF Reviews Stall

The reserve has passed 7,700 coins, two program reviews have slipped, and the daily buying goes on.

SAN SALVADOR — On the Pacific coast, a shopkeeper still takes bitcoin for a plate of pupusas and a cold bottle of water. Four years after the legal-tender law that made this country famous, the everyday scene is smaller than the headlines once promised — but the state’s own stack keeps growing.

The Strategic Bitcoin Reserve now holds more than 7,700 coins, worth roughly half a billion dollars, ranking El Salvador among the five largest sovereign holders on record.

That growth is the problem. Under its $1.4 billion IMF program, the government agreed to a hard ceiling of zero on new public-sector purchases. President Nayib Bukele keeps posting that the state buys one coin a day; the Fund insists the reserve is only shuffling coins between wallets it already controls. Two scheduled reviews — due last September and this March — have quietly slipped, with the next now expected before year-end.

Why it matters: the country that turned bitcoin into policy is now the test case for whether a government can hold it and satisfy its lenders at the same time.

The Take

Governments Regulate Bitcoin Only After Their Citizens Adopt It

Kenya wants a report on every trader. Indonesia handed its market to a new supervisor. Vietnam is licensing exchanges; Nigeria and Ghana wrote rulebooks after the fact. The sequence never reverses: people use bitcoin first — to save, to get paid, to move money past a broken bank — and the state writes the rules once the market it wants to tax already exists. Regulation here isn’t permission. It’s recognition, arriving late and calling itself a start.

Markets & Institutions

ABU DHABI. Bitcoin Suisse’s Middle East arm won full financial-services approval from Abu Dhabi’s regulator on July 7, clearing it to offer regulated trading, custody, and derivatives to institutions. The $3.7 billion Swiss custodian joins Binance and Kraken inside the ADGM free zone, and the permit follows a fresh EU license from Liechtenstein — a four-market footprint aimed squarely at Gulf money. Network & Mining

THIMPHU. Blockchain trackers flagged another 100 bitcoin, about $8 million, leaving Bhutan’s government wallets, pushing 2026 outflows past $230 million. The kingdom’s once-quiet stack has fallen below 3,200 coins from a 2024 peak near 13,000. Officials deny a fire-sale and call it reserve management — even as Gelephu, its new special zone, fast-tracks licenses for foreign crypto firms. Full story →

Policy & Nation-States

NAIROBI. Kenya’s Finance Bill would compel every licensed exchange to file user-level reports with the tax authority, part of a VASP regime advancing after last year’s crypto law. Local operators warn the compliance load favors global giants; the Treasury is chasing a market it counts in the hundreds of billions of shillings. Adoption came first; the paperwork is catching up. On the Ground

HARARE. On its sixth currency in fifteen years, Zimbabwe’s gold-backed ZiG is wobbling again, and savers are routing around it — into bitcoin and dollar tokens the moment a paycheck clears. The government says a de-dollarization roadmap is coming, alongside a 15% tax on money sent to offshore crypto platforms. Citizens have heard currency promises before. Full story →
