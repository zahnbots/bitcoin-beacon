A Peruvian Charity Now Runs Its Aid on Bitcoin

Motiv reaches 750 families a week across 15 Peruvian towns, teaching bitcoin as a plain payment tool — a circular economy built from a shoe drive.

CUSCO — The program that now moves bitcoin through fifteen Peruvian towns started with a pair of shoes. In 2019 a small charity called Motiv began fitting Andean children with boots sturdy enough for the walk to school. Six years on it reaches more than 750 families a week, teaches bitcoin six days a week across fifteen locations, and logged 25,000 to 30,000 bitcoin transactions from its 2025 events alone.

The pivot was an accident. In 2020, with donors refusing to redirect an earmarked shoe budget toward pandemic food, a bitcoin holder offered to fund it — on one condition: everything had to be bought in bitcoin. Motiv found merchants, kept a list, and taught them bitcoin as plumbing, not a bet.

The honest caveat is dependency: a 50-person, donor-seeded operation hasn’t shown the trade continues once the grants stop. What it has shown is narrower and real — bitcoin can reach people the banks skipped, with no price chart and no promise of riches.

Why it matters: the adoption that lasts rarely arrives by decree — it arrives when a merchant takes a coin because the person in front of them is holding one.

The Take

Bitcoin Is Splitting Into Two Economies

Read the week from two directions. Bottom-up: a Peruvian charity moving sats through 15 towns, Indonesians running 40 meetups a month. Top-down: an ETF liquidating into bigger rivals, Lightning capacity swelling from institutions, custody pooling into fewer hands.

Both are “adoption.” They point opposite ways. The number that settles it isn’t the price — it’s the ratio of people holding their own keys to assets parked with a few institutions. This week both rose.

On the Ground

JAKARTA. Indonesia banned bitcoin as payment in 2018, sending officers into shops that took it. Today it runs an estimated 40 meetups a month across 40 cities and a community near 55,000. The workaround is legal: don’t spend bitcoin, redeem it inside a closed-loop network — the structure that shields airline miles. Fragile, but it rebuilt one of the world’s most active scenes. Policy & Nation-States

HANOI. Vietnam is targeting the third quarter to open its first regulated crypto market, with five bank-linked firms cleared and a 0.1% trade tax. Bitcoin will be legal to trade — but still not to spend, with every settlement clearing in dong. In a country where 30%-plus already hold digital assets, the state is licensing the trade while keeping the money national. Full story →

Markets & Institutions

NEW YORK. A bitcoin ETF shut down. Hashdex’s DEFI fund stopped trading Aug 17 and began selling its 226 bitcoin, its $14.7M in assets too small to cover costs against fee-waiving giants. Demand for bitcoin isn’t the problem; a few mega-issuers have captured it. The coins stay in the market — the custody consolidates. Full story →

Network & Mining

THE NETWORK. Bitcoin’s Lightning Network holds more money than ever — public channels near 5,000 BTC — while its node count has slid from about 20,700 in 2022 to under 15,000. Capacity is rising because institutions are filling existing channels, not because new users are joining. A stronger payments layer, and a more concentrated one. Full story →
