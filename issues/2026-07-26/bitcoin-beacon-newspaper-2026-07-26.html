Indonesia Banned Bitcoin Payments. Its Users Found a Loophole.

You don’t spend the coin, you redeem it — and 55,000 Indonesians built a circular economy in the space the law forgot to close.

BANDUNG — Indonesia made paying in bitcoin illegal almost a decade ago, so its bitcoiners stopped calling it paying. Across roughly forty cities, a community now some 55,000 strong has built a national circular economy on one piece of legal jujitsu: at a member shop you don’t spend your bitcoin, you redeem it — the way you’d redeem loyalty points or airline miles.

Same coffee, same coin, different verb, and on the right side of a 2018 rule that still bars crypto as a means of payment. The plumbing comes from Fedi’s federated e-cash, with a federation estimated at 10,000 to 20,000 members; the classes are free, taught monetary-history-first by organizers who watched an earlier “Bitcoin Island” wave on Bali get crushed by the 2017 crackdown. The rupiah, down about 61% against the dollar over thirty years, does the recruiting.

Why it matters: adoption doesn’t wait for permission — and the closed-loop workaround is a template any country with a payments ban can copy.

The Take

Bitcoin Just Became a Tool of American Foreign Policy

On July 24 the State Department seated the Bitcoin Policy Institute inside a new “freedom tech” program — next to Palantir and Anduril. For an industry that spent a decade calling bitcoin freedom technology, that is real validation: money that escapes tyrants, funded and legitimized by Washington. It is also capture. Brand a stateless protocol an American instrument and rival governments gain a reason to treat it as hostile; a tool that works because it belongs to no one is a strange thing for any state to adopt. Both are true at once.
