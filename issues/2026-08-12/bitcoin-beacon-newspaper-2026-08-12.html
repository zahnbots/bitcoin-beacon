BlackRock Cuts Its Bitcoin-to-ETF Minimum From $25M to $1M

An in-kind swap once reserved for Wall Street desks now lets family offices move bitcoin into IBIT without triggering a taxable sale.

NEW YORK — BlackRock has cut the price of moving bitcoin into its IBIT exchange-traded fund without selling it. The minimum for an “in-kind” conversion — handing over actual bitcoin and receiving fund shares, no cash changing hands and no taxable sale triggered — drops from $25 million to $1 million, a 96% cut announced by digital-assets head Robbie Mitchnick on August 10.

The mechanism used to be the preserve of Wall Street trading desks. At $1 million it reaches registered advisers and family offices — the tier that holds appreciated coins and wants ETF convenience without a tax bill. Mitchnick says the threshold will keep falling, eventually to any size. It lands as IBIT’s five-day inflow streak snapped with a $144.67 million outflow: plumbing, not price, is where the contest sits.

Policy & Nation-States

WASHINGTON — The White House has given the Senate until September 15 to advance the CLARITY Act, the bill that would finally classify bitcoin as a commodity under the CFTC. Adviser Patrick Witt says if lawmakers “can’t get there by September 15, they never will.” Democrats blocked a pre-recess vote; a cloture motion now needs seven of them to cross. Network & Mining

Luke Dashjr has taken a sabbatical as chairman and CTO of the Jack Dorsey-backed mining pool Ocean, turning to the stalled BIP-110 fork he champions. His breakaway chain has produced just two blocks since splitting at block 961,632. Now its developers are drafting a hard fork that would abandon Bitcoin’s proof-of-work for a new algorithm, chosen by a secret commit-reveal to keep miners in the dark. Full story →

Sazmining, a hosted-mining provider, has become the first mining-as-a-service firm to accept Square — adding cards, Apple Pay, Cash App and ACH alongside bitcoin, Lightning and wire. The Take · Opinion

The ETF Owns Bitcoin. The Phone Spends It.

Today’s headlines came from downtown: a cheaper ETF conversion, a 3%-bitcoin portfolio, a bill to define the asset. All real, all legitimizing — and all about bitcoin the asset. The adoption that answers the original question, money for people the banks skipped, was somewhere else.

On Lightning, fees fall from 6–10% to a fraction of a cent. In Kenya, forty million M-Pesa numbers quietly became Lightning addresses this year; Machankura moves sats over USSD on phones with no internet. Wall Street decides what bitcoin is worth. A market stall in Lagos decides what it’s for.
