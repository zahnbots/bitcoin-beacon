Washington Wrote a Stablecoin Rulebook. Bitcoin Isn’t In It.

The Treasury's new GENIUS Act rules put licenses, bans and screening on stablecoin issuers - and underline the one digital asset that has no issuer to regulate.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · WASHINGTON · August 19, 2026 · 5 min read
Policy & Nation-States · A rulebook that presumes an issuer · Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

On August 17 the U.S. Treasury published its proposed rules for payment stablecoins under Section 3 of the GENIUS Act. It is a dense, consequential document — and reading it is the clearest way to see what bitcoin is by noticing everything in it that bitcoin isn’t.

The rules are built entirely around an issuer. Anyone issuing a payment stablecoin to Americans will need a state or federal license by January 18, 2027. Non-compliant foreign stablecoins face a ban on offers to U.S. persons from July 18, 2028. Token airdrops to U.S. residents are reclassified as formal issuances. Even answering an unsolicited inquiry from a U.S. customer now counts as a legal offer to sell. FinCEN and OFAC added joint rules requiring permitted issuers to run Bank Secrecy Act and sanctions-screening programs.

The asset with nobody to serve

Every one of those levers needs a company to pull it. A stablecoin is a liability: someone issues it, holds reserves against it, and can be licensed, audited, fined or shut down. That is what makes it regulable — and what the GENIUS rules methodically formalize.

Bitcoin has none of it. There is no issuer to license, no reserve to attest, no airdrop to reclassify, no corporate desk that can “offer” it, no head office to serve with a screening mandate. The rulebook that tightens around stablecoins has no chapter for bitcoin for a structural reason: bitcoin is a bearer asset, not somebody’s promise.

Every rule in the document presumes an issuer. That’s the one thing bitcoin doesn’t have.

Not unregulated — differently exposed

This is not a claim that bitcoin floats free of the law. The on-ramps are regulated: exchanges, custodians and brokers face licensing, KYC and tax reporting, and this week’s Citi custody news is a sign of how firmly bitcoin now sits inside supervised institutions. What the GENIUS rules govern — the issuance layer — simply doesn’t exist for bitcoin.

The honest counterpoint is that clear stablecoin rules are, on balance, good for the whole digital-asset economy: they legitimize dollar rails, push out bad actors, and give institutions a compliant on-chain dollar to work with. Stablecoins and bitcoin are complements more than rivals. But the asymmetry is real, and it explains a recurring pattern — when regulatory uncertainty rises, capital tends to favor the asset that no single rulemaking can redesign.

Why it matters: a rulebook is a map of where the control points are. The GENIUS Act shows how many of them stablecoins have — and how few of them bitcoin gives anyone to hold.
