Bond Yields Hit 2007 Highs. Bitcoin Held Its Ground.

As the 30-year Treasury yield touched levels unseen since 2007 and oil crossed $90, bitcoin stayed above $64,000 — and kept pulling money out of the rest of crypto.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · NEW YORK · August 19, 2026 · 6 min read
Money & Macro · Bonds sold off worldwide; bitcoin didn’t follow · Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

For most of bitcoin’s life, the easy way to describe it was “risk-on, with leverage.” When money got tight, it fell first and hardest. This week gave that description a hard test — and bitcoin, so far, has refused to play the part.

The backdrop was a genuine rout in the world’s safest assets. The United States 30-year Treasury yield climbed to its highest level since 2007. Germany’s 10-year Bund reached its highest since 2011, and Japan’s 10-year borrowing cost touched 3%. Brent crude pushed above $90 a barrel, up roughly 50% on the year. When the price of money and the price of energy rise together, every speculative asset is supposed to bleed.

Bitcoin did not. It opened Monday near $63,564, dipped to about $63,371 on pre-market selling, then climbed to $64,551 as North American buyers stepped in. Spot bitcoin ETFs took in close to $300 million on the day. By the time our ribbon was set this morning, River quoted bitcoin at $64,446.

A flight to quality — inside crypto

The more telling number is not the price but the share. Bitcoin’s dominance — its slice of the total crypto market — pushed to 59.3%. Capital is not leaving the asset class so much as consolidating inside it, draining out of smaller tokens and pooling in the one with the longest record and the deepest liquidity.

There is a mechanical reason for the rotation. Seventeen crypto projects are scheduled to unlock new supply between August 17 and 23, diluting holders at exactly the moment prices are soft. Faced with fresh issuance in the altcoins and none in bitcoin — whose supply schedule cannot be voted up — money moves toward the asset that can’t print more of itself.

Capital isn’t leaving crypto. It’s draining out of the tokens that can dilute you and pooling in the one that can’t.

On-chain data points the same way. Large wallets kept accumulating below bitcoin’s 200-week moving average — the long-run floor buyers have historically treated as a bargain line. That spot demand is what gave the market its buffer while bonds broke.

The case for caution

None of this proves bitcoin has severed its link to risk. The move up in yields has been orderly, not a panic; a genuine liquidity cascade — the kind that forces leveraged sellers to dump everything at once — would test the decoupling far harder than a grinding bond sell-off. Dominance, too, can reverse quickly: a risk-on melt-up tends to send money racing back into the smaller, faster-moving tokens, and bitcoin’s share falls as fast as it rose.

The chart still carries a warning. Bitcoin has broken above its descending trendline but has not confirmed a higher high; analysts peg a decisive breakout above roughly $65,500, with structural support down near $59,900 if the bounce fails. This is consolidation, not a breakout — a market holding its footing, not sprinting.

Still, the pattern matters because of what it rules out. Every bond rout that bitcoin shrugs off makes the “just leveraged tech beta” story a little harder to tell. The asset was designed for exactly this environment — rising deficits, higher-for-longer yields, currencies under strain — and this week it behaved like something investors reach for when the safe stuff stops feeling safe.

Why it matters: bitcoin’s value proposition was always a bet on what happens when sound money gets scarce. A week when the world’s bond markets cracked and bitcoin didn’t is a small, real data point in that bet’s favor.
