Wall Street Now Grades Bitcoin Miners on Power, Not Machines

CoinShares renamed its miner ETF around 'digital power' and rewrote its mandate to hold data centers and grid operators - a sign miners are now priced as energy companies.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · THE GRID · August 19, 2026 · 5 min read
Network & Mining · The market reprices miners as power companies · Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

The largest bitcoin-miner ETF just told you how Wall Street now values the people who secure the network: by the megawatt. On August 18, CoinShares renamed its “Bitcoin Miners ETF” (ticker WGMI) to the “Bitcoin Mining and Digital Power ETF.” The ticker still reads like an optimist’s slogan. The mandate underneath it now reads like a utility prospectus.

The rewrite is not cosmetic. The updated fund requires at least 80% of net assets in companies involved in bitcoin mining, data-center development, AI semiconductors, and power generation for high-performance computing. Investors, the fund’s own framing concedes, now evaluate these companies on their access to power connections rather than the number of machines they run.

Why the definition moved

The pressure is coming from the cost of building anything that draws serious electricity. This week BHP, the world’s largest mining company, reported that copper had overtaken iron ore as its top profit driver for the first time — $18 billion in copper profits, up 48% — and projected global copper demand rising from 34 million tonnes to 50 million by 2050, driven by AI data centers and power grids.

That demand makes the substations, transformers and transmission lines a bitcoin miner needs more expensive and harder to secure. So the public miners have diversified, leasing capacity to AI tenants and branding themselves as energy-infrastructure operators. The index is following the companies, not leading them.

The ticker still says “we’re gonna make it.” The mandate now says power plant.

What it costs bitcoin holders

For anyone who bought a miner ETF as a leveraged bet on bitcoin, the redefinition quietly changes the trade. A basket that must hold data centers, chipmakers and power generators is exposed to AI capital spending and grid economics as much as to the bitcoin price. The hashrate that secures the network is increasingly produced by firms whose investors are grading them on kilowatts, not coins.

The steelman is straightforward and strong: power access always was the real moat in mining, and diversification cushions the brutal swings of halvings and margin compression. A miner with a second revenue line is a more durable miner. But there is a cost to the category losing its edges — the “bitcoin miner” as a clean proxy for the network is blurring into a general bet on electrified compute. This is distinct from a single company’s AI pivot; it is the benchmark itself redrawing the line.

Why it matters: the businesses that secure bitcoin are being repriced as power companies. How they’re financed shapes where the hashrate goes — and who ends up owning the machines that keep the network running.
