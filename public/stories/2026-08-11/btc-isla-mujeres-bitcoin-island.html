On Isla Mujeres, Sixteen Shops Now Take Bitcoin

Two years ago the count was zero — now a one-woman project is turning a Cancún ferry island into a Lightning economy.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · ISLA MUJERES · August 11, 2026 · 6 min read
On the Ground · A Lightning economy, one stall at a time · Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

On a strip of limestone eight kilometres long, a ferry ride from Cancún, a taco, a night’s lodging, or a golf-cart tour can now be paid for in bitcoin. Two years ago none of that was possible. The change has a name: BTC Isla, a not-for-profit run by Isabella Santos, who moved to Isla Mujeres in early 2024 to find out whether a small island economy could be rebuilt on sound money from the bottom up.

The scoreboard is modest and precise. Of several hundred businesses on the island, sixteen now accept bitcoin over the Lightning Network. At the start of 2024, that number was zero. Most of the sixteen are food-and-drink vendors, but the list already reaches beyond restaurants to lodging, transport, and tours — the everyday purchases that decide whether a currency is money or a novelty.

How the flywheel starts

Circular economies do not begin with technology; they begin with a person willing to knock on doors. Santos onboards merchants one at a time, installs point-of-sale tools and a bitcoin ATM, and — the part that actually moves adoption — teaches. BTC Isla runs free classes in a Bitcoin Café, each built around five short sessions and paired with a welcome dinner at a local restaurant. The project charges no fees and takes no cut. It is funded as a gift of time and money, not a business.

The pedagogy matters because the failure mode of merchant adoption is not signup, it is churn. A shop that accepts bitcoin once and never sees a second payment quietly turns the feature off. The fix is a loop: locals earn a few sats, spend them at a merchant who understands why he is holding them, and that merchant pays a supplier or an employee in kind. The goal is to keep value circulating on the island without a detour through pesos.

The failure mode of merchant adoption is not signup. It is the second payment that never comes.

Why an island

Isla Mujeres is small enough to be legible and touristic enough to have dollars, pesos, and now sats flowing through it every day. That is the same recipe that made El Zonte, El Salvador — “Bitcoin Beach” — the template every circular-economy project cites. Bitcoin Beach itself has backed the island’s effort, granting BTC Isla two million sats in seed support as it got going.

The tourist trade is a feature, not a distraction. Visitors arrive already holding bitcoin and looking for somewhere to spend it; a Lightning payment clears in seconds for a fraction of a cent, sparing a foreign traveler the currency-conversion haircut and the merchant the card-processing fee. Each spend is also a demonstration to the vendor that the thing works.

The honest limits

Sixteen merchants is not an economy. It is a proof of concept on an island whose commerce runs overwhelmingly on cash and cards, and whose residents have no particular reason to abandon the peso they already trust. Adoption at this scale is fragile: it depends heavily on one organiser, a handful of committed shopkeepers, and a steady trickle of bitcoin-holding tourists. Remove any of the three and the loop slows.

What makes it worth a dateline is the direction, not the magnitude. A year and a half took the island from zero to sixteen, from no classes to a standing weekly curriculum, from an idea to an ATM on the ground. Nation-states announce bitcoin policy in press conferences; places like Isla Mujeres build it purchase by purchase, which is the version that tends to survive a change of government.

Why it matters: the adoption that lasts is built shop by shop, not decreed from a capital.
