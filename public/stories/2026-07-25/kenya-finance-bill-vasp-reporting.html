Kenya Wants a Report on Every Bitcoin Trader

The Finance Bill would force exchanges to file user-level records with the tax office as a licensing regime takes shape.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · NAIROBI · July 25, 2026 · 6 min read
Nairobi: the paperwork catches up to the market — Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

Kenya is one of the most bitcoin-active countries on the continent, a place where peer-to-peer trading and mobile-money rails carry billions in value each year with almost no formal oversight. The state is now moving to change that — not by banning anything, but by demanding a paper trail. The Finance Bill 2026 would compel every licensed virtual-asset service provider to report detailed, user-level information to the tax authority.

The mechanism is a targeted amendment to the Tax Procedures Act. Exchanges and trading platforms — VASPs, in the regulatory shorthand — would have to file annual information returns to the Kenya Revenue Authority detailing their dealings with Kenyan customers, the "reportable users." It is a reporting mandate first and a revenue tool second: the state wants to see the market before it decides how hard to tax it.

The wider net

The bitcoin provision travels with a broader push to tax digital money. The 2026 bill pairs the VASP reporting rules with new levies on digital payments, including withholding taxes on card transactions, and analysts have tied the package to a KSh-billions revenue target. Kenyan authorities have publicly framed the crypto flow they want to capture in the trillions of shillings — a sign the Treasury sees digital assets as a meaningful base rather than a fringe.

This does not arrive in a vacuum. Kenya passed a VASP law in 2025, and the National Treasury concluded stakeholder consultations on draft VASP regulations in April 2026. The Finance Bill's reporting mandate is the enforcement muscle behind that framework: rules on the books mean little without a way to see who is doing what.

The state wants to see the market before it decides how hard to tax it.

The complaint from the industry

Local operators have not welcomed all of it. Blockchain-industry voices have warned that heavy compliance and reporting burdens could favor large foreign platforms with the legal and technical resources to absorb them, squeezing smaller Kenyan firms out of a market they helped build. It is a familiar tension: rules written for legibility can entrench the incumbents best equipped to comply.

There is also the practical reach problem. Much of Kenya's bitcoin activity happens peer-to-peer and on offshore exchanges precisely because they sat outside the formal system. A reporting mandate binds the licensed platforms; it does not automatically capture the trades that route around them. The bill narrows some of those escape valves, but enforcement against genuinely decentralized activity remains the hard part.

Adoption first, rules after

The sequence is the tell. Kenyans did not wait for a legal framework to start using bitcoin — they adopted it to send remittances, hedge the shilling, and move money faster than the banking system allowed. The state is now catching up to a market that already exists, writing the rules that let it observe and tax what its citizens have been doing for years. That is not a Kenyan quirk; it is the pattern across the region, from Lagos to Accra.

The bottom line

Kenya's Finance Bill would turn its licensed exchanges into reporting agents for the tax office, the enforcement layer on top of a 2025 VASP law. It legitimizes the market and surveils it in the same stroke. Whether it broadens the tax base or simply pushes activity further into peer-to-peer channels depends on how far the reporting net can actually reach.
