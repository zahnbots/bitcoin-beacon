Russia Opens Bitcoin Trading to the Public

The central bank cleared bitcoin for licensed exchanges — but capped ordinary Russians at about $3,600 a year, and only after they pass a test.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · MOSCOW · August 17, 2026 · 6 min read
Policy & Nation-States · Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

For years the Bank of Russia was one of bitcoin’s loudest official skeptics, pushing at times for an outright ban on trading and mining. On August 11 it did the opposite. The regulator added bitcoin, ether and Tether’s USDT to the short list of assets that Russians will be allowed to buy and sell on licensed domestic exchanges. Trading begins September 1.

The move follows the law President Vladimir Putin signed on August 4, which legalizes regulated crypto trading under central-bank supervision. The core provisions take effect this September; rules governing the issuance and wider circulation of digital assets are scheduled to follow a year later, on September 1, 2027. After a decade of treating bitcoin as a threat to the ruble, the Russian state has decided it would rather host the market than chase it offshore.

A market on a leash

The opening is deliberately narrow. Russians classified as “unqualified” investors — the ordinary public — will be able to hold no more than 300,000 rubles, roughly $3,600, in these assets through any single broker, exchange or asset manager. Before their first trade, every investor must pass a test on the risks involved.

“Qualified” investors — those who clear income or asset thresholds — face no such ceiling and may buy any of the approved assets without limit. The structure mirrors how Moscow already gates other high-risk instruments: available to everyone in principle, rationed by wealth in practice.

The list itself is short and pointed. Only three tokens were cleared for public exchange trading: bitcoin, ether and USDT. XRP, widely traded in Russia’s grey markets, did not make the cut — a reminder that the regulator, not the user, is choosing what counts.

After a decade of treating bitcoin as a threat, the Russian state decided it would rather host the market than chase it offshore.

Why Moscow changed its mind

The reversal is inseparable from sanctions. Cut off from Western card networks and correspondent banking since 2022, Russia has spent two years searching for payment rails it does not have to ask permission to use. Earlier in 2026 it legalized bitcoin and other crypto for cross-border trade settlement, letting exporters and importers move value outside the dollar system. Bringing retail onshore is the domestic half of the same strategy: build deep, taxable, watchable pools of crypto liquidity at home rather than let that activity run through foreign platforms the Kremlin cannot see.

For the state, a licensed exchange is an instrument of visibility. Trades clear through registered intermediaries, identities are known, and flows can be measured — the opposite of the peer-to-peer and offshore trading that dominated Russian crypto for years. What looks like liberalization is also surveillance by another name.

What it means for bitcoin

Strip away ether and the stablecoin and the bitcoin-specific fact is straightforward: the government of one of the world’s most sanctioned economies has just told more than 140 million citizens they may legally own bitcoin through a regulated venue. Even a $3,600 annual ceiling, multiplied across a large population and layered with unlimited access for the wealthy, is a meaningful new source of onshore demand.

It is also a case study the Beacon has watched play out elsewhere, from Vietnam’s licensed exchanges to Brazil’s withdrawal holds: states are learning that the way to manage bitcoin is not to forbid it but to route it through a front door they control. The coin that was designed to need no permission is increasingly being handed out with a permission slip.

The steelman for Moscow’s approach is real. A regulated market gives Russians consumer protections, recourse against fraud, and a legal path to an asset that holds its value better than a ruble under sanctions. The catch is the same one that shows up wherever access arrives through a licensed intermediary: an account the state can see is an account the state can freeze. Russians gain a legal way to buy bitcoin, and the government gains a ledger of who did.

Why it matters: the country most isolated from the dollar is bringing bitcoin onshore — on the state’s terms, not the holder’s.
