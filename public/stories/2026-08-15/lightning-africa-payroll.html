Lightning Now Pays Salaries Across Africa

Bitcoin’s payment layer has quietly become a payroll and remittance rail from Lagos to Nairobi — reaching feature phones, and mostly through apps that hide the bitcoin entirely.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · LAGOS · August 15, 2026 · 7 min read
On the Ground · Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

The fastest-growing use of Bitcoin’s Lightning Network in 2026 is not speculation, and it is not tipping. It is payroll. Bitnob, a Nigerian-founded payments company, now runs Lightning-based salary payments for remote workers across 23 African countries, and the volume it moves has grown 340% in a year, according to a June assessment from the wallet-infrastructure firm Spark.

The mechanics are deliberately dull. An employer abroad pays in dollars; the money crosses borders as bitcoin over Lightning — settling in seconds for a fraction of a cent — and lands with a worker as local currency. The recipient often never sees a bitcoin balance at all. Bitcoin is the wire, not the destination.

Reaching the phones that can’t reach the internet

The harder problem in African payments is not fees; it is hardware. Most bitcoin apps assume a smartphone and a data plan. Machankura removed that assumption. It lets people send and receive Lightning payments over USSD and SMS — the menu-driven codes that work on a $15 feature phone with no internet — across Ghana, Kenya, Malawi, Nigeria, South Africa and Uganda. A user dials a short code, navigates a text menu, and moves value that settles on the same network a Wall Street desk uses.

Kenya turned every mobile-money number into a bitcoin address

The most aggressive experiment is in Kenya. Since May, the payments app Tando has made every mobile number connected to M-Pesa — roughly 40 million of them — automatically reachable as a Lightning address in the form number@bitcoin.co.ke. A sender anywhere in the world can pay that address in bitcoin; Tando converts it to M-Pesa credit the recipient spends at a shop counter, with no fee on Tando’s side. Bitcoin becomes the settlement layer under Africa’s dominant mobile-money system without asking the user to learn a single new habit.

The network is growing and shrinking at once

Zoom out and Lightning looks paradoxical. Public capacity sat near 4,898 BTC across 41,080 channels in May, and monthly volume crossed $1.17 billion last November — up 266% year over year, with about 12 million transactions a month. Yet the number of public nodes has fallen to 17,438, down from a 2022 peak near 20,700. More money is moving over fewer independent machines.

That is the shape of the African rollout too. Bitnob, Machankura and Tando are custodial or heavily abstracted: they hold keys, manage channels and hide the plumbing so an ordinary person never confronts it. Convenience is the product. The network is consolidating around service providers, not spreading node by node.

Bitcoin is winning Africa as a wire, not as a wallet people hold themselves.

The trade-off nobody advertises

For a worker paid in a currency that lost a third of its value, a custodial Lightning wallet that holds dollars for a week and settles instantly is a genuine upgrade. But it is an upgrade with a counterparty. The same abstraction that onboards millions also reintroduces the intermediary bitcoin was built to remove — a company that can freeze an account, get hacked, or be leaned on by a regulator. The Kenyan grandmother reachable at her phone number is not running a node; she is trusting Tando.

That is not a reason to dismiss the progress. It is the reason to watch what comes next: whether these rails stay custodial funnels, or whether the people they onboard ever take hold of their own keys. Adoption is real. Sovereignty is still optional.

Why it matters: bitcoin is becoming everyday money across Africa as a payment rail, not as savings people control — and it is arriving through apps that hide the coin.
