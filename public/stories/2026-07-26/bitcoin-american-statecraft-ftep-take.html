Bitcoin Just Became a Tool of American Foreign Policy

A bitcoin policy group now sits inside the State Department's freedom-tech program. Recognition this big always cuts both ways.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · WASHINGTON · July 26, 2026 · 5 min read
Recognition is also capture: bitcoin enters statecraft — Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

On July 24 the U.S. State Department launched a "Freedom Tech Excellence Program," a scheme to embed private-sector specialists inside the department on short assignments tied to digital freedom. Its four founding partners tell you where Washington's head is: the Bitcoin Policy Institute, the data-analytics firm Palantir, the defense contractor Anduril, and the Victims of Communism Memorial Foundation. A bitcoin advocacy group now has a seat at the statecraft table, next to two of the most powerful surveillance-and-defense companies in America. That is a milestone. It is also a warning.

Start with the milestone, because it is real. For a decade bitcoiners have argued that a censorship-resistant, permissionless money is freedom technology — a way for dissidents, journalists and ordinary people under capital controls to move value past a hostile state. The program's remit runs to exactly that terrain: countering unlawful surveillance, defending encryption and privacy tools, protecting free expression online. Having the U.S. government treat bitcoin as part of that toolkit, rather than as a speculative nuisance to be regulated, is a validation the industry has wanted for years.

The case for cheering

The optimistic reading is straightforward. Bitcoin's most defensible use has never been getting rich in Miami; it has been getting money out of Caracas, Lagos or Yangon when the banks are weapons. If the State Department is prepared to fund and legitimize the tools that make that possible — and to put bitcoin's advocates in the room where those decisions are made — that is resources and cover for a genuinely humanitarian use of the network. Recognition from the world's most powerful government is not nothing.

Bitcoin's best argument — money that escapes tyrants — is now also an argument the tyrants' rivals want to own.

The case for worrying

Now the warning. Look again at the guest list. Palantir and Anduril are not freedom-tech companies; they are surveillance and defense contractors whose business is building the very capabilities dissidents fear. A program that files bitcoin under "freedom tech" alongside them is not neutral framing — it is capture. It risks turning a stateless protocol into an instrument of one state's foreign policy, and the moment bitcoin is branded an American tool, rival governments gain a reason to treat it as a hostile one. A protocol that works precisely because it belongs to no one is a strange thing for any government to adopt as its own.

There is a subtler cost, too. Freedom tech's credibility rests on being genuinely apolitical — useful to a Hong Kong protester and a Russian ophthalmologist and a Sudanese refugee without asking whose side they are on. Wrap it in a great-power program and you hand every authoritarian a talking point: see, it was always their weapon. The tool doesn't change. The story around it does, and stories get people arrested.

Holding both

The honest position is that both things are true at once. This is real legitimacy and real co-option risk, and which one dominates depends on details not yet visible: whether the program funds open, neutral tools or bespoke instruments; whether bitcoin's advocates shape the agenda or decorate it. Bitcoiners who spent a decade asking to be taken seriously by Washington are about to learn the price of being taken seriously by Washington.

The bottom line

Bitcoin entering U.S. statecraft is a win the industry lobbied for and a risk it should have seen coming. The network doesn't need a sponsor; its power is that it has none. The test is whether Washington's embrace strengthens freedom tech or quietly converts it into leverage — and whether bitcoin's people are shaping the program or being used by it.
