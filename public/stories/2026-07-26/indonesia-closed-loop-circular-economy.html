Indonesia Banned Bitcoin Payments. Its Users Found a Loophole.

A payments ban pushed Indonesia's bitcoiners into a closed loop: you don't spend the coin, you redeem it — and merchants can legally accept it.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · BANDUNG · July 26, 2026 · 7 min read
Indonesia: at the warung, you redeem bitcoin rather than spend it — Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

Indonesia made paying in bitcoin illegal almost a decade ago. So its bitcoiners stopped calling it paying. Across roughly forty cities, a community that now counts some 55,000 people has built a national circular economy on a single piece of legal jujitsu: you do not spend your bitcoin at a member shop, you redeem it — the way you would redeem airline miles or a store's loyalty points. Same coffee, same coin, different verb, and on the right side of the law.

The distinction is not a joke. Bank Indonesia's 2017 regulation, effective January 2018, barred cryptocurrencies — and even foreign banknotes like dollars and euros — as instruments of payment, citing money-laundering and financial-stability risk. It was enforced. Businesses that advertised bitcoin acceptance drew undercover checks, and some were shut under threat of arrest. What the rule never touched was ownership: holding bitcoin as an asset stayed legal. Only its use as a medium of exchange was off-limits.

The verb that unlocked a market

That gap is the whole game. A closed-loop rewards scheme — credit-card points, frequent-flyer miles — is not legally a currency, because value only circulates among members and approved merchants. Indonesia's bitcoiners borrowed the structure. Inside their network, members earn and redeem bitcoin with each other and with participating stores; nobody is "paying" in the regulatory sense, so merchant acceptance opens up without daring the ban.

The plumbing comes from Fedi, whose federated e-cash ("Fedimint") mints let a community custody and move small balances with strong privacy. Bitcoin Indonesia's federation is estimated at 10,000 to 20,000 members — deliberately fuzzy, because e-cash is private by design. Alongside it, wallets like Blink and Wallet of Satoshi handle Lightning payments for anyone who wants self-custody or cross-border reach.

Same coffee, same coin, different verb — and on the right side of the law.

How the network got rebuilt

This is Indonesia's second bitcoin wave, and the founders remember the first. Around 2014 a "Bitcoin Island" experiment on Bali drew international press and, by the community's own count, grew from about 42 merchants to a few hundred — capped by the sale of a luxury villa for roughly 800 BTC, then worth about $500,000. The 2017 crackdown, arriving alongside a bull market that briefly pushed on-chain fees toward $50, snuffed most of it out. Adoption went dormant for years.

The comeback was engineered after COVID by a small group of Indonesian bitcoiners, among them Dimas Alfaruq, a former materials engineer who had run paid crypto-education events for exchanges before deciding the model was backwards. Their pitch inverted it: education would be free. Where competing "crypto academies" charged $1,000 to $2,000 and seeded expectations of quick riches, Bitcoin Indonesia gave the classes away and taught monetary history first — what money is, why the rupiah keeps failing, how a saver breaks the cycle.

The rupiah is the recruiter. It has lost about 61% of its value against the dollar over thirty years, a slow erosion an island nation of 280 million people feels in the price of rice and school fees. A population that already distrusts its currency is, as the organizers put it, fertile ground.

By the numbers

  • ~55,000 people engaging with bitcoin through Bitcoin Indonesia's efforts.
  • ~40 meetups a month across roughly 40 cities; all free to attend.
  • 10,000–20,000 members in the Fedimint e-cash federation (estimate).
  • 500 graduates of the My First Bitcoin course by 2025, with a goal to double by end-2026.
  • ~61% fall in the rupiah vs. the dollar over 30 years.

Meetups as infrastructure

The scale — about forty gatherings a month — runs on a template the group wrote down and posted publicly on GitHub. Its rules are unglamorous and, that is the point, repeatable: the organizer must actually understand bitcoin; must commit to leading long-term, not hosting once; must secure a genuinely free venue, ideally a cafe with no minimum order; and receives flags, books, flyers and stickers from the center, printed locally and reimbursed. The brand promise is three words: free, free, free.

That structure has made Bitcoin Indonesia one of the larger nodes of My First Bitcoin, the certification program born in El Salvador, and it shows up online too — roughly 27,000 followers on Instagram, 10,000 on TikTok, a few thousand on Telegram, most content in a mix of Bahasa Indonesia and English. The effort has drawn support from the Human Rights Foundation, OpenSats, Block, and Mike Peterson of Bitcoin Beach.

Why it matters

Indonesia sits near the top of global grassroots-adoption rankings, and the reason isn't a friendly regulator — it's a hostile one, routed around. The closed-loop model is a template any country with a payments ban can copy: keep custody and circulation inside a members' network, call it redemption, and let merchant acceptance grow in the space the law forgot to close. It is slower than legal tender and clumsier than an app store, but it is durable, because it doesn't ask permission.

The risk is equally clear. A loophole exists until a regulator decides to close it, and Indonesia is mid-way through moving crypto oversight to its financial-services authority, a transition that could rewrite the rules the workaround depends on. For now, the verb holds. In a warung in Bandung, a bowl of noodles changes hands, a QR code is scanned, and no one has broken the law.

The bottom line

Indonesia is the clearest case that adoption doesn't wait for permission. Told it couldn't spend bitcoin, a 55,000-strong community rebuilt the market as a loyalty program and kept going. The state's next move — how it supervises crypto from here — will decide whether the loophole stays open or the network has to adapt again.
