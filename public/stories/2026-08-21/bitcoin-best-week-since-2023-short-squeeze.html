Bitcoin Tops $79,000 in Its Best Week Since 2023

A Treasury plumbing change lit the fuse; a wall of crushed short bets did the rest. The move is real — the demand under it is still thin.

By The Bitcoin Beacon · NEW YORK · August 21, 2026 · 6 min read
Money & Macro · The rally ran on fiscal plumbing and forced buyers, not a fresh crowd of holders · Illustration: The Bitcoin Beacon

Bitcoin does not usually rip because of a debt-management memo. This week it did, twice over. The coin climbed as high as $79,400 on Friday, up roughly 8.8% since midnight UTC and nearly 24% since Monday — its strongest weekly advance since March 2023. It was trading just below $78,000 by midday; a read of River's live page at 13:49 UTC showed $77,353.

The spark was the U.S. Treasury. On Wednesday it moved to expand its long-dated bond buybacks, and the bid never let up: five straight up days, each one dragging the next batch of doubters into the trade. What began as a macro nudge turned into a mechanical stampede.

Not QE, but liquidity all the same

Bond buybacks are not quantitative easing and not yield-curve control. The Treasury is not printing money; it is managing its own debt, retiring older long-dated paper. But the effect at the margin rhymes with easing: a larger official buyer supports bond prices, nudges long yields down, and softens the dollar. The Dollar Index sat at 98.77, off its August highs. Lower yields and a softer dollar are a tailwind for an asset that pays no coupon, and risk appetite did the rest.

The squeeze inside the rally

Leverage supplied the accelerant. As price rose, exchanges force-closed traders positioned for a fall; each liquidation is itself a forced purchase, which lifts price and triggers the next. Roughly $3.3 billion in derivatives positions were wiped out Wednesday, and a further $1.24 billion over the following 24 hours — shorts accounting for $1.06 billion of that, and 152,586 traders liquidated in all. The single largest order was a $23.59 million bitcoin position on Hyperliquid.

What makes the setup unusual is that traders keep betting against it. The aggregated long-short account ratio sat at 0.865 — more accounts short than long — even after four days of being wrong. Open interest climbed 6.17% to $139.37 billion across the market, with bitcoin futures OI up 7.38% to $57.7 billion. Predicted funding rates ticked to 0.013%, the highest since January. The fuel for further squeezing is only partly spent.

The gains were built on liquidations, not on a visible wave of new spot buyers — the tell of a squeeze, not a mania.

Is anyone actually buying?

Two demand signals point up, tentatively. U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs took in $606 million on August 20, up from $517 million the day before; counting ether funds, the two-day haul topped $800 million. And CryptoQuant's 30-day apparent spot-demand measure has clawed back from minus 206,000 BTC on July 23 to roughly minus 5,000 — on the cusp of turning positive for the first time since February 26. When that gauge crosses from negative to positive, bitcoin has historically delivered a median 18% gain over the next 60 days with a 78% hit rate; the figures rise to 23% and 87% when it fires with the MVRV ratio below its 365-day average, as it is now.

The caution is that the signal has not completed and the sample is small. The firm's own work finds the same zero-cross in futures demand carries almost no forward edge. So the sustainability of this move rests on whether the spot bid that emerged behind the squeeze holds once the forced covering stops.

How durable is the pop?

Squeeze-driven rallies tend to give ground once the forced buyers are done. Bitcoin has already cleared the $76,000 level implied by the inverse head-and-shoulders pattern that had formed since June, which means the traders who bought that break have hit their target — and the relative strength index is in overbought territory. A short-term pullback would be ordinary. The bullish read is simpler: the Treasury has signaled it will lean against a disorderly rise in long yields, and lower yields favor scarce assets.

Why it matters: the number on the screen jumped because Washington adjusted how it buys its own bonds — not because a new wave of people decided to hold bitcoin.
